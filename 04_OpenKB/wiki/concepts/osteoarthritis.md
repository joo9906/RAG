# Osteoarthritis

## Definition
Osteoarthritis (OA) is the **most common form of arthritis**, also known as **degenerative arthritis** or **degenerative joint disease**. It is a chronic disorder in which **articular cartilage** gradually wears away, exposing the underlying bone and altering surrounding joint tissues. OA commonly causes **pain**, **functional limitation**, and progressive **structural joint loss**.

OA may be **primary (idiopathic)**, when no single cause is identified, or **secondary**, when joint damage from another condition leads to cartilage breakdown.

## Etymology
The term *osteoarthritis* comes from:
- **Osteo** = bone
- **Arthro** = joint
- **-itis** = inflammation

Although the suffix suggests inflammation, OA is primarily a **degenerative process** with secondary inflammatory changes.

## Core Pathophysiology
In OA:
1. **Joint cartilage deteriorates** over time.
2. **Subchondral bone** becomes exposed as cartilage is lost.
3. The **synovial membrane** may become inflamed.
4. These changes lead to **pain**, reduced function, stiffness, and **joint deformity**.

Structural progression is often reflected on imaging, where cartilage loss, bone remodeling, and joint space narrowing become increasingly visible.

## Typical Symptoms and Functional Limitation
OA commonly presents with mechanical joint symptoms:
- **Pain related to joint use or activity**
- **Joint stiffness**, especially **morning stiffness lasting less than 30 minutes**
- **Reduced range of motion**
- **Joint swelling**
- **Crepitus** or joint sounds
- **Joint deformity** in advanced disease

These findings reflect progressive structural and functional impairment and help distinguish OA from inflammatory arthritides such as [[concepts/rheumatoid-arthritis]].

## OA vs. Rheumatoid Arthritis
OA is often differentiated from [[concepts/rheumatoid-arthritis]] by its clinical pattern:
- Usually occurs in **older adults**, especially after **age 50**
- Associated with **aging** and **joint overuse**
- Tends to affect **weight-bearing joints** and is often **asymmetric**
- Usually **localized** rather than systemic
- **RF** is generally **negative**, and **ESR** may be only transiently elevated

By contrast, RA often begins earlier, is **symmetric**, involves **small joints**, and shows more clearly systemic inflammatory markers.

## Epidemiology and Joint-Specific Burden
OA is highly prevalent in older adults, but burden differs substantially by joint and by sex.

### Population-based prevalence patterns in Korean adults
A national survey-based study of adults aged 50 years or older found that **symptomatic spinal OA** was present in:
- **5.6% of men**
- **16.0% of women**

The same study showed that women had higher proportions of **symptomatic OA** and **symptomatic+radiographic OA** across several sites, especially the knee and spine.

A separate cross-sectional study in adults aged 65 years or older found that radiographic OA was most common in the:
- **Spine**
- **Hand**
- **Knee**
- **Shoulder**
- **Hip**

These findings highlight that OA is not limited to the knee and should be understood as a **joint-specific and systemic age-related burden**. This is relevant to [[concepts/spinal-osteoarthritis]] and to treatment selection across different anatomical sites.

### Joint-specific symptom burden
OA burden can vary by location:
- **Knee OA** often causes walking difficulty and load-related pain
- **Hand OA** may affect grip and fine motor function
- **Hip OA** can impair gait and mobility
- **Spinal OA / spondylosis** can produce back pain, stiffness, and nerve-related symptoms when stenosis develops

This broader perspective is especially important because OA should be understood as a **multi-joint degenerative disease with site-specific clinical burden**, not only as knee disease.

## Spondylosis as a Form of OA
The document on **spondylosis** describes it as **spinal osteoarthritis**, a degenerative change of the spine. It is caused by age-related damage to bones, discs, cartilage, and ligaments, and may present with **low back pain**, **stiffness**, or no symptoms at all.

When spinal degeneration narrows the spinal canal, **spinal stenosis** can occur, leading to **numbness**, **muscle weakness**, or other nerve compression symptoms. Diagnosis relies on history, physical exam, and imaging such as **X-ray**, **MRI**, and **CT**. Treatment follows the general OA principle of relieving pain and improving function through non-drug measures, medications, and sometimes surgery.

This supports the view that OA includes **spine involvement** as part of a broader degenerative joint spectrum, linking [[concepts/spinal-osteoarthritis]] to the overall OA framework.

## Diagnosis and Imaging
Diagnosis is based on a combination of **history**, **physical examination**, **clinical findings**, and **imaging**, rather than a single test alone. Early OA may show few abnormalities, so clinical suspicion remains important.

### X-ray findings
- Early-stage OA may appear normal
- As disease progresses, **joint space narrowing** becomes visible
- **Subchondral sclerosis** may develop
- Advanced disease may show **osteophytes** and an irregular joint surface

### MRI findings
MRI can assess:
- cartilage thickness
- cartilage volume
- biochemical composition of cartilage

MRI is useful for detecting early cartilage change, but routine use is limited by **cost** and **time**.

### OA-related research and MRI-based assessment
OA research uses imaging and symptom measures to evaluate disease burden and progression. Common endpoints include **VAS**, **K-WOMAC**, **dGEMRIC**, **WORMS**, **BME**, and **MRI**. These terms are important in interpreting OA clinical studies and connect OA evaluation to [[concepts/mri-based-joint-assessment]], [[concepts/cartilage-biomarkers]], and [[concepts/patient-reported-outcome-measures]].

## Causes and Risk Factors
### Primary osteoarthritis
The exact cause of primary OA is not known, but contributing factors include:
- Aging
- Sex
- Genetic factors
- Obesity

### Secondary osteoarthritis
Secondary OA occurs when **articular cartilage is damaged by an underlying cause**, such as:
- Trauma
- Disease
- Structural deformity

Examples include OA following **bacterial arthritis**, **tuberculous arthritis**, severe impact, or repeated minor trauma.

## Treatment Principles
Management of OA aims to:
- reduce pain
- preserve function and prevent deformity
- improve quality of life

These goals align with a combined strategy of [[concepts/nonpharmacologic-treatment]] and symptom-directed medication in [[concepts/osteoarthritis-management]].

### Guideline-based management
The **2019 ACR guideline** for hand, knee, and hip OA organizes treatment into **strongly recommended** and **conditionally recommended** interventions. Key recommendations include:
- **Exercise**
- **Weight loss**
- **NSAIDs**
- **Intra-articular steroid injections**

This highlights the central role of [[concepts/nonpharmacologic-treatment]] together with pharmacologic symptom control.

### Non-pharmacologic treatment
Core non-drug measures include:
- exercise
- weight control
- muscle strengthening

These are foundational for long-term symptom control and functional maintenance.

### Pharmacologic treatment
Common medication approaches include:
- **Topical agents**: NSAIDs and related preparations
- **Oral agents**: acetaminophen, NSAIDs, COX-2 inhibitors
- **Intra-articular injections**: hyaluronic acid, steroids

Current therapies primarily target **symptoms**, not the underlying disease process.

## OA Drug Classifications
OA treatments are often grouped into three categories:

### SYRADOA
**Symptomatic Rapid Acting Drugs in OA**
- acetaminophen
- NSAIDs
- intra-articular corticosteroids

These drugs provide relatively fast symptom relief.

### SYSADOA
**Symptomatic Slow Acting Drugs in OA**
- glucosamine/chondroitin sulfate
- avocado/soybean unsaponifiables
- diacerin
- **Joins®**

These agents are used for slower, more sustained symptomatic benefit.

### DMOADs
**Disease Modifying OA Drugs**
- agents intended to reduce or alter structural joint changes

No currently established therapy has been proven to prevent or reverse OA-related structural degeneration, which underscores the unmet need for true [[concepts/disease-modifying-osteoarthritis-drugs]].

## Joins® in Osteoarthritis
Related Joins studies have examined both **safety** and **analgesic efficacy** in osteoarthritis.

A post-marketing surveillance study of **Joins tablets** in osteoarthritis / degenerative joint disease reported an overall adverse event rate of **3.02%** among **5,962 patients**, with events not ruled out as drug-related occurring in **2.38%**. The most common adverse reactions were **gastrointestinal**, including:
- heartburn
- dyspepsia
- epigastric discomfort
- gastrointestinal disorder
- epigastric pain

Less frequent reactions involved the **general/systemic**, **skin**, **blood**, **psychiatric**, **autonomic**, **hepatic/biliary**, and **central/peripheral nervous systems**, including edema, headache, rash, leukopenia, insomnia, palpitations, AST/ALT elevation, and dizziness. This is relevant to [[concepts/adverse-reactions]] and [[concepts/drug-safety]] in OA treatment with [[concepts/Joins]].

A randomized controlled trial in **knee osteoarthritis** compared **JOINS 200 mg tid** with **diclofenac SR 100 mg qd** over **4 weeks** in **249 patients**. The study used **VAS** as the primary endpoint and **Lequesne index** plus global satisfaction scores as secondary endpoints. JOINS showed **similar clinical efficacy** to diclofenac, with **better safety and tolerability**.

A separate OA phase 3 comparative trial further supported this pattern by directly comparing JOINS with diclofenac on **adverse reaction outcomes**. In that study, the **JOINS® group (n=125)** had a drug-related adverse reaction rate of **19.2%**, while the **diclofenac group (n=124)** had a rate of **37.1%**; the difference was **statistically significant** (**p=0.017**, chi-square test). This indicates that JOINS had a **lower adverse reaction rate** than diclofenac in OA patients and reinforces its role as a symptomatic treatment option within [[concepts/osteoarthritis-management]].

Another OA phase 3 comparative trial focused on pain outcomes and found that after 4 weeks, the **JOINS group** showed a **VAS** change of **-14.18 ± 17.53 mm**, while the **diclofenac group** showed **-15.49 ± 15.37 mm**; the difference was **not statistically significant** (**p = 0.53**). Together, these data suggest that JOINS provides **analgesic effects comparable to diclofenac** while offering a **potential safety advantage**.

A newer Joins-related OA study adds structural evidence: in a **1-year phase 4 cartilage-protection trial**, JOINS® maintained the **dGEMRIC index** over 1 year, whereas the placebo group showed a significant decline. Because the dGEMRIC index reflects **cartilage GAG content**, this result suggests that JOINS may help preserve cartilage composition in OA and supports a possible [[concepts/cartilage-protection]] effect alongside symptomatic benefit.

The document summarized here adds another important long-term clinical finding: in a **1-year OA phase 4 trial**, JOINS® showed **significant improvement versus placebo** in both **VAS** and **K-WOMAC**. This supports a sustained benefit for **pain reduction** and **physical function** in OA, complementing the earlier dGEMRIC-based structural signal.

## Joins® and Systemic OA Burden
Because OA affects multiple joints, not only the knee, some Joins-related materials frame its use more broadly across **degenerative joint disease** and **spinal OA**. This matters for understanding **reimbursement scope** and clinical positioning, especially when comparing products limited to **knee OA** with products used in a wider OA spectrum. The inclusion of [[concepts/spinal-osteoarthritis]] in this framework reflects OA as a broader degenerative condition rather than a single-joint disorder.

## Key Features
- Most common type of arthritis
- Progressive wear of articular cartilage
- Exposure and remodeling of underlying bone
- Associated synovial inflammation
- Can result from age-related degeneration or direct joint injury
- Leads to pain and structural changes in the joint
- Often causes **activity-related pain** and **brief morning stiffness**
- May produce **joint sounds**, **swelling**, and **range-of-motion limitation**
- Typically **asymmetric** and localized to **weight-bearing joints**
- RF is usually negative; ESR may be only transiently elevated
- X-ray may be normal early, with later **joint space narrowing**, **sclerosis**, and **osteophytes**
- MRI provides more detailed cartilage assessment but is less practical for routine use
- OA clinical studies often use **VAS**, **K-WOMAC**, **WORMS**, **BME**, and **dGEMRIC** to assess symptoms and structure
- Treatment is largely symptom-directed, with no proven disease-modifying drug yet
- The 2019 ACR guideline emphasizes **exercise**, **weight loss**, **NSAIDs**, and **intra-articular steroid injections**
- Joins® is included among **SYSADOA** agents and has post-marketing safety data showing mostly mild, gastrointestinal adverse reactions
- In OA trials, JOINS has shown **comparable efficacy to diclofenac** and, in one phase 3 study, a **significantly lower adverse reaction rate**
- In a 1-year OA phase 4 study, JOINS maintained the **dGEMRIC index**, suggesting a potential cartilage-preserving effect
- In another 1-year OA phase 4 study, JOINS improved **VAS** and **K-WOMAC** versus placebo, supporting long-term symptomatic benefit
- OA is not limited to the knee; it can affect the **spine**, **hip**, and **hand joints**, which is relevant to [[concepts/reimbursement-scope]] and treatment selection
- Some Joins-related materials emphasize that Joins® may be reimbursable across **systemic degenerative joint disease**, including **spinal osteoarthritis**, unlike products limited to **knee-only OA** indications

## Source Context
This concept synthesizes multiple source documents covering OA as a foundational degenerative joint disease, its major causes and risk factors, characteristic symptom patterns, diagnostic methods, treatment goals and drug classes, the **2019 ACR guideline** for OA management, post-marketing adverse reaction data for **Joins®**, and OA-related clinical research endpoints such as **VAS**, **K-WOMAC**, **dGEMRIC**, **WORMS**, **BME**, and **MRI**. It also incorporates OA phase 3 comparative evidence showing **JOINS and diclofenac produced similar pain reduction on VAS**, while a newer study adds that **JOINS had a significantly lower drug-related adverse reaction rate than diclofenac** and a **1-year dGEMRIC preservation signal**.

The newer comparison document also adds an important practical framing: OA is a **body-wide degenerative joint disease**, not only a knee disorder. That distinction helps explain why **Joins®** may be discussed as having broader reimbursement applicability across **spine and other joints**, whereas some competing products are limited to **knee OA** only.

## Practical Significance
OA is clinically important because it is common, chronic, and associated with pain and loss of joint function. Understanding the distinction between **primary OA**, linked to aging, genetics, and obesity, and **secondary OA**, following cartilage injury from trauma, infection, or deformity, clarifies why OA is classified as a degenerative joint disease rather than a purely inflammatory arthritis.

The symptom profile of **activity-related pain**, **short-duration morning stiffness**, **joint sounds**, and **restricted motion** is especially characteristic. Imaging supports diagnosis by showing progressive structural change: **X-ray** is useful for later-stage joint space loss, sclerosis, and osteophytes, while **MRI** better evaluates cartilage but is less practical in routine care.

Treatment is primarily aimed at **symptom relief and functional preservation**. Exercise, weight control, and strengthening are key non-drug measures, while medications such as NSAIDs, acetaminophen, injections, and SYSADOA agents like **Joins®** are used to reduce pain. The 2019 ACR guideline supports a structured approach in which **exercise**, **weight loss**, **NSAIDs**, and **intra-articular steroid injections** are central options, with treatment strength varying by joint site and intervention. However, a proven **disease-modifying osteoarthritis drug** has not yet been established.

OA research also relies on standardized outcome measures. Patient-reported endpoints such as **VAS** and **K-WOMAC** capture pain and function, while imaging endpoints such as **WORMS**, **BME**, and **dGEMRIC** capture structural and biochemical cartilage changes. These measures help connect clinical symptoms with the underlying degenerative process and are important for evaluating therapies in [[concepts/osteoarthritis-clinical-outcomes]].

The Joins studies add direct comparative evidence that JOINS can achieve **VAS pain reduction similar to diclofenac** in OA and may also produce **fewer adverse drug reactions**. The new 1-year dGEMRIC result extends this by suggesting a **possible cartilage-preserving effect**, while the new 1-year VAS/K-WOMAC result supports **sustained clinical improvement in pain and function**. Together, these findings strengthen its position as a symptomatic OA therapy with potential structural relevance.
