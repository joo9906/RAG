# Post-Infectious Joint Damage

## Definition
Post-infectious joint damage refers to structural injury of a joint, especially articular cartilage, that occurs after an infection has affected the joint. When the infection is severe enough to destroy cartilage, the joint may later develop degenerative changes such as [[concepts/osteoarthritis]] or contribute to [[concepts/secondary osteoarthritis]].

## Relationship to the source document
The summary page [[summaries/2_1_1 24년 하반기 신입사원_조인스_이재은_학술교재_0006]] notes that **secondary (acquired) osteoarthritis** can result from diseases that damage joint cartilage, including:
- **Bacterial arthritis**
- **Tuberculous arthritis**

In these cases, infection is not just a temporary inflammation; it can permanently damage cartilage and alter joint mechanics, creating a pathway to later degeneration.

## Key features
- **Cause:** Infection involving the joint, especially septic or tuberculous arthritis
- **Mechanism:** Inflammatory destruction of articular cartilage and joint structures
- **Outcome:** Residual joint damage, deformity, pain, stiffness, and increased risk of OA
- **Clinical importance:** A history of joint infection is a risk factor for later chronic joint disease

## Why it matters
Infectious arthritis can leave long-term sequelae even after the infection is controlled. The resulting cartilage loss and joint surface irregularity may produce ongoing symptoms or predispose the joint to accelerated wear. This makes post-infectious damage a classic example of [[concepts/joint damage]] leading to [[concepts/secondary osteoarthritis]].
