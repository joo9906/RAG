# Site-Specific Osteoarthritis Prevalence

Site-specific osteoarthritis prevalence refers to how often osteoarthritis (OA) appears in different anatomical locations such as the spine, hand, knee, hip, or shoulder. This concept is important because OA is not a single uniform condition: its burden, symptoms, and radiographic findings differ substantially by joint and by population.

## Why this matters

OA prevalence can look very different depending on:
- **The joint being examined**
- **The age group studied**
- **Whether prevalence is based on symptoms, imaging, or both**
- **Sex differences** in disease distribution

This makes site-specific OA a useful concept for understanding [[concepts/osteoarthritis]] as a heterogeneous disease rather than one diagnosed uniformly across all joints.

## Key patterns from the source document

The source document [[summaries/2_1_1 24년 하반기 신입사원_조인스_이재은_학술교재_0040]] compiles Korean prevalence studies showing clear variation by site.

### 1) National survey of adults aged 50+ years
A population-based study using the Korean National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (2010–2012) found that symptomatic spinal OA was especially common, and that women had higher combined symptom-plus-radiographic prevalence across major joints.

Reported patterns included:
- **Spine**: high burden, especially in women
- **Knee**: common in both sexes, with higher burden in women
- **Hip**: comparatively low radiographic OA overall

This study also distinguished between:
- **Sx** = symptoms only
- **OA** = radiographic features only
- **Sx+OA** = both symptoms and radiographic features

That distinction is central to [[concepts/radiographic findings]] and to interpreting OA prevalence accurately.

### 2) Cross-sectional study of adults aged 65+ years
In older adults, the highest radiographic OA prevalence was found in the **spine**, followed by the **hand**, **knee**, **shoulder**, and **hip**.

Approximate overall prevalence among assessed participants:
- **Spine**: 66.4%
- **Hand**: 60.0%
- **Knee**: 38.1%
- **Shoulder**: 5.2%
- **Hip**: 2.2%

### Sex differences
The studies suggest that prevalence is not evenly distributed between men and women:
- **Women** tend to show higher prevalence of **hand** and **knee** OA.
- **Spinal OA** is highly prevalent in both sexes and may be particularly prominent in older adults.
- **Hip OA** is consistently less common than OA in the spine, hand, or knee.

These patterns connect to [[concepts/sex differences in disease prevalence]] and broader [[concepts/epidemiology]] of musculoskeletal disease.

## Core idea

The main takeaway is that OA prevalence depends strongly on anatomical site. The spine, hand, and knee account for much of the observed burden, while hip and shoulder OA are less common in the cited studies. Because prevalence varies by site, research and clinical screening should avoid treating OA as a single, site-independent condition.

## Practical implications

Site-specific prevalence data help with:
- Estimating population burden
- Identifying high-risk joints for screening and prevention
- Comparing clinical symptoms with imaging evidence
- Designing targeted treatment and public health strategies
