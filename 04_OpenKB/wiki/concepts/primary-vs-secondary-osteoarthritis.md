# Primary vs Secondary Osteoarthritis

## Definition
**Primary vs secondary osteoarthritis (OA)** classifies OA by whether joint degeneration arises from an **unclear, multifactorial origin** or from a **specific underlying cause** that damages the joint. This distinction is useful in OA epidemiology because prevalence and presentation vary by joint, age, and sex, as shown in [[summaries/2_1_1 24년 하반기 신입사원_조인스_이재은_학술교재_0040]].

## Primary osteoarthritis
Primary OA, also called **idiopathic OA**, has no single confirmed cause. Instead, it is thought to develop through a combination of risk factors over time:

- **Age**
- **Sex**
- **Genetic factors**
- **Obesity**

In this form, the joint degeneration is not explained by a clear prior injury or disease.

Primary OA is often the category used in population studies of [[concepts/osteoarthritis]] when no specific secondary cause is identified. These studies commonly report higher burden in older adults and sex differences across sites such as the spine, hand, knee, and hip.

## Secondary osteoarthritis
Secondary OA occurs when a **known condition or event directly injures articular cartilage** or alters joint structure. Common causes include:

- **Trauma**
- **Disease** affecting the joint
- **Deformity** or structural abnormality

Examples from the source include:

- Cartilage destruction after **bacterial arthritis**
- Cartilage destruction after **tuberculous arthritis**
- OA following **severe impact**
- OA following **repeated minor trauma**

Secondary OA is important in cases where prior joint damage can be traced to a specific event or disorder, fitting closely with [[concepts/cartilage injury]], [[concepts/trauma]], [[concepts/joint damage]], and [[concepts/post-infectious joint disease]].

## Key distinction
The main difference is the presence of an identifiable cause:

- **Primary OA**: cause is not clearly identified; risk factors contribute over time
- **Secondary OA**: caused by a specific insult, disease, or deformity

## Clinical and epidemiologic relevance
This classification helps explain **how OA develops** and supports more accurate assessment of contributing factors such as [[concepts/risk factors]], [[concepts/trauma]], and [[concepts/cartilage injury]]. It also helps interpret prevalence data, since studies like [[summaries/2_1_1 24년 하반기 신입사원_조인스_이재은_학술교재_0040]] show that OA burden differs substantially by joint site and sex, with the spine, hand, and knee often showing the highest rates in older adults.

In practice, distinguishing primary from secondary OA can clarify whether disease is likely driven by aging and systemic risk factors or by localized structural damage from prior disease or injury.
