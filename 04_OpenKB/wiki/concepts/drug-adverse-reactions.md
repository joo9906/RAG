# Drug Adverse Reactions

## Definition
Drug adverse reactions are unwanted, unintended, harmful, or otherwise undesirable effects that occur during medication use. They may be recorded as **adverse events** when any unwanted medical occurrence is observed, or as **adverse reactions** when a causal link to the medicine is possible or likely.

## Why this concept matters
Adverse reactions are central to evaluating **drug safety** and **tolerability**, especially in clinical trials and after a product enters the market. They help clinicians, regulators, and patients understand:
- how often unwanted effects occur,
- which body systems are most affected,
- how severe or treatment-limiting the effects may be,
- and whether one treatment is better tolerated than another.

This concept is closely related to [[concepts/post-marketing-surveillance]] and [[concepts/drug-safety]]. It is also important in comparative studies that assess both efficacy and safety, such as [[concepts/randomized-controlled-trials]] and chronic pain or arthritis treatment studies.

## Key terms
- **Adverse event**: Any unwanted medical occurrence during treatment, whether or not it is caused by the drug.
- **Adverse reaction**: A reaction where the drug is considered possible or likely to be involved.
- **Causality assessment**: Evaluation of whether the drug may have contributed to the event.
- **Tolerability**: The extent to which side effects are acceptable or manageable during treatment.
- **System-organ classification**: Grouping reactions by affected body system, such as gastrointestinal, skin, or nervous system.

## How adverse reactions are used in evaluation
Adverse reaction data are commonly used in two ways:
1. **Descriptive safety profiling** — documenting which reactions occur and how frequently they appear.
2. **Comparative tolerability assessment** — comparing treatments to determine whether one has fewer or less burdensome unwanted effects than another.

This makes adverse reaction reporting a core endpoint in both [[concepts/post-marketing-surveillance]] and [[concepts/randomized-controlled-trials]].

## Example from the Joins documents
The Joins source summaries illustrate adverse reactions in both **post-marketing surveillance** and **comparative clinical trial** settings.

### Post-marketing surveillance example
The source summary [[summaries/2_1_1 24년 하반기 신입사원_조인스_이재은_학술교재_0020]] describes post-marketing surveillance data for **Joins tablets (조인스정)** in patients with osteoarthritis / degenerative joint disease.

#### Reported rates
- Overall adverse events: **3.02% (180/5,962)**
- Events that could not be ruled out as drug-related: **2.38% (142/5,962)**

#### Most common affected systems
The document shows that reactions were reported across multiple systems, with **gastrointestinal effects** being the most frequent:
- heartburn
- dyspepsia
- epigastric discomfort
- gastrointestinal disorder
- epigastric pain

Other reported systems included:
- general/systemic reactions such as edema and headache
- skin reactions such as pruritus and rash
- blood-related reactions such as leukopenia
- psychiatric reactions such as insomnia
- autonomic symptoms such as palpitations
- hepatic/biliary changes such as AST/ALT elevation
- nervous system symptoms such as dizziness

### Comparative trial example
The source summary [[summaries/2_1_1 24년 하반기 신입사원_조인스_이재은_학술교재_0024]] reports a randomized controlled trial comparing **JOINS** with **diclofenac** in patients with knee osteoarthritis.

#### Safety finding
- JOINS showed **similar clinical efficacy** to diclofenac over 4 weeks.
- JOINS demonstrated **better safety and tolerability** than diclofenac.

### Additional comparative safety evidence
The source summary [[summaries/2_1_1 24년 하반기 신입사원_조인스_이재은_학술교재_0026]] adds another osteoarthritis phase 3 comparison of **JOINS®** and **diclofenac** focused specifically on adverse reactions.

#### Reported rates
- JOINS® adverse reaction rate: **19.2% (n=125)**
- Diclofenac adverse reaction rate: **37.1% (n=124)**
- Difference: **statistically significant** (**p=0.017**, chi-square test)

This study strengthens the safety comparison by showing that adverse reaction assessment can quantify the relative tolerability of two treatments, not just the presence of side effects.

### Rheumatoid arthritis comparative trial example
The source summary [[summaries/2_1_1 24년 하반기 신입사원_조인스_이재은_학술교재_0027]] reports an RCT comparing **JOINS** with **celecoxib** in patients with rheumatoid arthritis.

#### Safety and tolerability finding
- JOINS had **noninferior pain-relief efficacy** compared with celecoxib.
- **Tolerability was similar** between the two groups over 6 weeks.

### Rheumatoid arthritis phase 3 adverse reaction comparison
The source summary [[summaries/2_1_1 24년 하반기 신입사원_조인스_이재은_학술교재_0029]] provides a direct phase 3 safety comparison of **JOINS** and **celecoxib** in RA.

#### Reported rates
- **JOINS group (n=91)**: adverse reactions in **41.8%**
- **Celecoxib group (n=92)**: adverse reactions in **39.1%**
- **p = 0.7171**

#### Drug-related adverse reactions
- JOINS: **29.7%**
- Celecoxib: **23.9%**
- **p = 0.3792**

#### Interpretation
The trial found **no statistically significant difference** between the two groups in either overall adverse reactions or drug-related adverse reactions. This supports the idea that JOINS and celecoxib have **comparable safety profiles** in RA treatment, reinforcing its role in [[concepts/rheumatoid-arthritis]] and [[concepts/drug-safety]] discussions.

### RA indication and non-inferiority context
The source summary [[summaries/2_1_1 24년 하반기 신입사원_조인스_이재은_학술교재_0039]] adds an important RA-specific positioning point: **JOINS** is presented as reimbursable for **rheumatoid arthritis**, while **Imotone®** is described as having an indication only for **knee osteoarthritis**.

#### RA phase 3 efficacy evidence
- **Study type:** RCT
- **Objective:** Compare pain relief and tolerability of JOINS® vs. celecoxib in RA patients
- **Participants:** RA patients, **n = 183**
- **Intervention:**
  - **JOINS 200 mg tid**
  - **Celecoxib 200 mg bid**
- **Duration:** 6 weeks
- **Endpoints:**
  - **Primary:** VAS (pain score)
  - **Secondary:** rescue medication use frequency, **20% improvement response rate**
- **Conclusion:** JOINS was **non-inferior to celecoxib for pain relief**, with **similar tolerability**

#### Relevance to adverse reactions
Although this trial summary is primarily about efficacy, its tolerability result reinforces the broader adverse-reaction theme: in RA, JOINS is not only supported by comparative efficacy evidence but also by a **safety and tolerability profile comparable to celecoxib**. This makes the document relevant to both [[concepts/rheumatoid-arthritis]] and [[concepts/non-inferiority-trials]].

## Interpretation
This concept captures how unwanted effects are identified and interpreted in both routine pharmacovigilance and clinical research. Post-marketing surveillance can reveal side effects not fully captured in pre-approval trials, while comparative studies help determine whether one treatment has a more favorable safety profile than another.

In the Joins materials, adverse reaction data consistently support the idea that safety evaluation should include both **incidence** and **comparative burden** across treatments, especially in chronic-use conditions such as [[concepts/osteoarthritis-treatment]] and inflammatory joint disease such as [[concepts/rheumatoid-arthritis]]. The RA-specific evidence from [[summaries/2_1_1 24년 하반기 신입사원_조인스_이재은_학술교재_0039]] further shows that tolerability is an important part of product positioning alongside indication and efficacy.
