# Biologic Therapy

Biologic therapy refers to the use of **biologic agents** that specifically target molecules involved in immune and inflammatory pathways. In autoimmune diseases such as rheumatoid arthritis (RA), these drugs are used to reduce inflammation by blocking key mediators rather than broadly suppressing the immune system.

## Core idea

Biologic agents are designed to interfere with defined components of the inflammatory cascade. In RA, this helps suppress the disease process, reduce joint inflammation, and limit further damage.

## Key points from the source document

The source summary on [[summaries/2_1_1 24년 하반기 신입사원_조인스_이재은_학술교재_0014]] describes biologic therapy as part of RA treatment, noting that:

- biologic agents can block the **intermediate substances** that drive rheumatoid inflammation
- **TNF inhibitors** are a major example of this class
- examples given include **infliximab** and **adalimumab**
- biologics are used to help control inflammation and support disease management

## Relationship to rheumatoid arthritis treatment

In RA, biologic therapy is typically considered when more targeted control of inflammation is needed. It is part of the broader treatment framework alongside [[concepts/NSAIDs]], [[concepts/corticosteroids]], and [[concepts/DMARDs]].

- [[concepts/NSAIDs]] mainly relieve pain and inflammation symptoms
- [[concepts/corticosteroids]] provide strong anti-inflammatory effects
- [[concepts/DMARDs]] help slow progression and may induce remission
- [[concepts/biologic-therapy]] provides targeted immune modulation

## Examples of biologic agents

Common biologic therapies mentioned in the source include:

- **Infliximab**
- **Adalimumab**

These are both **TNF inhibitors**, meaning they block tumor necrosis factor (TNF), a major inflammatory signaling molecule.

## Why biologic therapy matters

Biologic therapy is important because it offers:

- **targeted suppression** of inflammatory pathways
- improved control of autoimmune inflammation
- potential to reduce joint injury and disease progression in RA
