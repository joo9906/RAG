# Osteoarthritis Risk Factors

Osteoarthritis risk factors are characteristics or conditions that make OA more likely to develop or become clinically apparent, especially in **primary (idiopathic) osteoarthritis**. The source material distinguishes between **primary OA**, whose exact cause is unclear, and **secondary OA**, which results from direct cartilage damage due to injury, disease, or deformity.

This concept also includes epidemiologic patterns showing that OA burden varies by **age**, **sex**, and **joint site**. Newer prevalence data from Korean adult studies show that OA is especially common in the **spine**, **hand**, and **knee**, with notable sex differences in [[concepts/sex differences in disease prevalence]] and [[concepts/joint-specific osteoarthritis]].

## Core risk factors for primary osteoarthritis

The document identifies several major factors believed to influence **primary osteoarthritis**:

- **Age**: Risk increases with aging.
- **Sex**: Biological sex influences OA prevalence and phenotype.
- **Genetic factors**: Inherited susceptibility may contribute.
- **Obesity**: Excess body weight is associated with higher OA risk.

These factors do not necessarily directly cause OA on their own, but they increase vulnerability to joint degeneration over time.

## Epidemiologic patterns by age and sex

The added prevalence data show that OA is not distributed evenly across the population:

- In adults **50 years and older**, symptomatic spinal OA was reported in **5.6% of men** and **16.0% of women**.
- In adults **65 years and older**, radiographic OA was most common in the **spine (66.4%)**, followed by the **hand (60.0%)**, **knee (38.1%)**, **shoulder (5.2%)**, and **hip (2.2%)**.
- Women tended to have higher rates of **hand** and **knee** OA, while spinal OA was also highly prevalent in both sexes.

These findings support the idea that OA risk is shaped not only by individual susceptibility but also by age-related accumulation of joint changes and site-specific mechanical or biological stress.

## Relationship to secondary osteoarthritis

Secondary osteoarthritis is not primarily defined by general risk factors, but by **specific causes of cartilage injury**. The source highlights:

- **Trauma** to the joint
- **Disease** affecting the joint
- **Structural deformity**
- Prior **bacterial arthritis** or **tuberculous arthritis** that destroys cartilage
- Severe impact or repeated minor trauma

These are better understood as direct causes or triggers of joint damage rather than background risks.

## Why these factors matter

Understanding OA risk factors helps distinguish between:

- **Primary OA**: multifactorial, with no single clear cause
- **Secondary OA**: attributable to identifiable joint damage

The prevalence findings also show that OA burden depends on whether one measures **symptoms**, **radiographic findings**, or both. In particular, the gap between symptomatic disease and radiographic OA is important for interpreting patient complaints and imaging findings in [[concepts/osteoarthritis]].

This distinction is important for:

- assessing patient history
- evaluating joint symptoms
- interpreting imaging results
- considering prevention and early intervention strategies
