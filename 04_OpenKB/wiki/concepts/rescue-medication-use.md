# Rescue Medication Use

## Definition
**Rescue medication use** refers to the need for an additional, as-needed medication given when the assigned study treatment does not fully control a patient's symptoms. It is commonly used in clinical trials as a **secondary efficacy measure** because lower use suggests better symptom control.

## Role in Clinical Trials
Rescue medication use helps researchers assess whether a treatment provides adequate relief beyond the primary endpoint. In pain-focused studies, frequent rescue medication use may indicate incomplete pain control, while reduced use may support stronger treatment effectiveness.

This endpoint is especially useful when evaluating treatments for chronic painful conditions because it captures whether patients still require extra symptom relief during the study period, complementing measures such as [[visual analog scale]] (VAS).

## Example from the JOINS RA Studies
In the rheumatoid arthritis (RA) study of [[JOINS]] versus [[celecoxib]], rescue medication use was included as a **secondary endpoint** alongside VAS and response rate.

### Study context
- **Study type:** [[randomized controlled trial]]
- **Population:** Rheumatoid arthritis patients
- **Intervention:** [[JOINS]] 200 mg tid
- **Comparator:** [[celecoxib]] 200 mg bid
- **Duration:** 6 weeks
- **Primary endpoint:** VAS
- **Secondary endpoints:** frequency of rescue medication use, 20% improvement response rate

## Interpretation in the RA Study
The inclusion of rescue medication use indicates that the trial assessed not only measured pain scores, but also whether patients needed extra symptom relief during treatment.

In this RA study, [[JOINS]] showed **non-inferior pain relief** compared with [[celecoxib]], and tolerability was similar. In that context, rescue medication use served as a practical marker supporting the overall assessment of treatment adequacy and symptom control.
