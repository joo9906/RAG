# X-Ray Findings in Osteoarthritis

X-ray is a core imaging tool for evaluating **[[concepts/osteoarthritis|osteoarthritis (OA)]]**, especially when structural joint changes have progressed enough to become visible on plain radiographs. It helps support diagnosis by showing **anatomic and degenerative changes** in the joint, while also providing a practical way to compare OA burden across sites in population studies.

## Core radiographic findings

According to the source document [[summaries/2_1_1 24년 하반기 신입사원_조인스_이재은_학술교재_0008]], the main X-ray findings in OA are:

- **Early disease may appear normal**
  - In the initial stages of OA, radiographs can look unremarkable.
  - A normal X-ray does not exclude OA.

- **Joint space narrowing**
  - As cartilage is lost, the space between bones becomes reduced.
  - This is one of the most important visible signs of progressive OA.

- **Subchondral sclerosis**
  - The bone beneath the cartilage becomes denser and appears more opaque on X-ray.
  - This reflects increased stress on the underlying bone.

- **Osteophyte formation**
  - In more advanced OA, bony outgrowths develop at the margins of the joint.
  - These are commonly described as bone spurs.

- **Irregular joint surface**
  - With progression, the joint surface becomes uneven and less well defined.
  - This reflects ongoing degeneration and remodeling.

## X-ray and OA prevalence by joint

A later source, [[summaries/2_1_1 24년 하반기 신입사원_조인스_이재은_학술교재_0040]], adds epidemiologic context by showing how often radiographic OA is detected in different joints among Korean adults.

### Population-based prevalence patterns

In adults aged **65 years or older**, radiographic OA was most common in the **spine**, followed by the **hand**, **knee**, **shoulder**, and **hip**:

- **Spine**: 66.4% (462/696)
- **Hand**: 60.0% (415/692)
- **Knee**: 38.1% (265/696)
- **Shoulder**: 5.2% (36/696)
- **Hip**: 2.2% (15/686)

This reinforces that X-ray-detectable OA is highly site-dependent and that axial and peripheral joints show different patterns of disease burden.

### Sex differences in radiographic OA

The same study found sex-specific differences:

- **Women** had higher prevalence of **hand** and **knee** OA.
- **Men** had slightly higher prevalence of **spinal** OA in some comparisons.

Among adults aged **50 years or older** in a national survey, symptomatic spinal OA was also more common in women than men:

- **Men**: 5.6%
- **Women**: 16.0%

The survey also distinguished among:

- **Sx** = symptoms only
- **OA** = radiographic features only
- **Sx+OA** = both symptoms and radiographic features

These categories show that X-ray findings may not perfectly match symptoms, which is important when interpreting OA severity and clinical impact.

## Why X-ray matters

X-ray is useful because it is widely available, relatively inexpensive, and good for identifying structural joint damage. In OA, it is especially helpful for showing later-stage changes such as **joint-space loss**, **sclerosis**, and **osteophytes**.

It is also useful for understanding how OA burden differs by joint and by sex, as reflected in population studies of [[concepts/epidemiology]] and [[concepts/joint-specific osteoarthritis]].

However, X-ray has an important limitation: it may not detect early cartilage damage before structural changes become visible. For that reason, it is often interpreted together with clinical findings and sometimes with **[[concepts/mri|MRI]]**.

## Diagnostic context

X-ray findings should not be used in isolation. OA diagnosis typically combines:

- patient history
- physical examination
- radiographic findings
- other imaging when needed

This reflects the broader **[[concepts/diagnostic-workup|diagnostic workup]]** approach described in the source.
