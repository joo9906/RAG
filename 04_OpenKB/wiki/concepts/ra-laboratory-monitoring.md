# Laboratory Monitoring in Rheumatoid Arthritis

## Overview
Laboratory monitoring in rheumatoid arthritis (RA) supports both **disease assessment** and **treatment safety**. It does not replace clinical evaluation or diagnostic criteria, but it helps clinicians estimate inflammatory activity, monitor medication toxicity, and check for possible organ involvement.

This is especially important because RA management often involves long-term use of anti-inflammatory and disease-modifying therapies, including [[concepts/DMARDs]], [[concepts/biologic-therapy]], [[concepts/NSAIDs]], and [[concepts/corticosteroids]].

## What is monitored

### Inflammatory activity
Two common markers are used to estimate the level of inflammation:

- **ESR (erythrocyte sedimentation rate)**
- **CRP (C-reactive protein)**

These tests are often elevated when inflammatory activity is present. In RA, they are part of the broader diagnostic framework and also help track disease activity over time. See also [[concepts/inflammatory-markers]].

### Treatment safety and organ involvement
Routine blood tests are used to monitor for complications from therapy and possible systemic involvement:

- **CBC / general blood tests**
- **Liver function tests**
- **Kidney function tests**

These tests are important because RA medications may cause adverse effects, and RA itself or its complications can involve other organs. This monitoring is especially relevant in long-term management. See also [[concepts/lab-monitoring-in-rheumatoid-disease]].

## Relationship to RA diagnosis
Laboratory tests are only one part of RA evaluation. The source documents describe diagnosis as a score across four domains:

1. Joint involvement pattern
2. Serologic testing
3. Acute-phase reactants such as ESR and CRP
4. Symptom duration

A total score of **6 or more** supports an RA diagnosis. Laboratory markers therefore contribute to classification, but they are not sufficient alone. For the diagnostic framework, see [[concepts/rheumatoid-arthritis-diagnosis]].

## Rheumatoid factor in context
The documents also discuss **rheumatoid factor (RF)**. RF is positive in many RA patients, but it can also be positive in healthy individuals, so it is not diagnostic by itself. A higher RF level may correlate with more severe joint damage. This makes serology useful, but still only one piece of the full evaluation. See [[concepts/autoantibody-testing]].

## Link to treatment goals
Laboratory monitoring supports the main goals of RA treatment: preventing joint damage, suppressing inflammation, preserving daily function, and reducing complications. By tracking ESR and CRP, clinicians can judge whether anti-inflammatory control is improving. By checking blood, liver, and kidney tests, they can watch for toxicity from therapies used to achieve these goals.

## Practical takeaway
Laboratory monitoring in RA serves three main purposes:

- **Estimate inflammation** with ESR and CRP
- **Monitor medication toxicity** with blood, liver, and kidney tests
- **Support overall RA assessment** alongside symptoms, joint findings, and serology
