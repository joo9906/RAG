# Diagnostic Workup

## Definition
A diagnostic workup is the structured process of evaluating a patient by combining **medical history**, **physical examination**, **laboratory tests**, and **imaging studies** to reach or confirm a diagnosis.

## Core idea
Rather than relying on a single finding, diagnostic workup brings together multiple sources of evidence to improve diagnostic accuracy. This is especially important when early disease may appear subtle, symptoms are nonspecific, or one test alone cannot confirm the condition.

## Key components
- **History taking**: clarifies symptom onset, duration, progression, triggers, and functional impact.
- **Physical examination**: identifies clinical signs that support the suspected condition.
- **Laboratory tests**: help support diagnosis, assess inflammation, and monitor for other organ involvement or treatment effects.
- **Imaging studies**: help confirm structural changes, assess disease severity, or rule out alternative diagnoses.
- **Integrated interpretation**: all findings are weighed together, not in isolation.

- joint involvement pattern
- serologic testing
- acute-phase reactants such as ESR and CRP
- symptom duration

A total score of **6 points or more** supports RA diagnosis. The document also emphasizes that **rheumatoid factor is not sufficient on its own**, because it can be positive in some healthy people and is only present in about 80% of RA patients. This reinforces the need for a stepwise, multi-source diagnostic approach.

- symptom history and progression
- physical examination
- X-ray to assess arthritis or bone damage
- MRI to evaluate the spinal cord and surrounding nerves
- CT to examine the spinal canal, bones, and joints

This source reinforces that degenerative spine disease may require multiple imaging modalities, especially when evaluating for **spinal stenosis** or nerve compression.

## Why it matters
A complete diagnostic workup helps clinicians:
- avoid missing early disease
- interpret test results in context
- distinguish among similar conditions
- choose the most useful next test
- confirm disease using a combination of clinical and objective findings

## Related imaging and testing roles
In the source documents, imaging and laboratory testing each play a distinct role in the workup:

- **X-ray** is useful for detecting structural joint changes such as joint space narrowing, subchondral sclerosis, osteophytes, and bone damage as disease progresses.
- **MRI** provides more detailed information about cartilage thickness, volume, and biochemical composition, and in spine disease it helps assess the spinal cord and nerve compression, but it is limited by cost and time.
- **CT** is helpful for evaluating the spinal canal, bones, and joints in structural spine disorders.
- **ESR** and **CRP** help assess inflammation and disease activity.
- **Routine blood tests** such as CBC, liver function tests, and kidney function tests help evaluate treatment safety and possible systemic involvement.
- **Serologic tests**, including **rheumatoid factor**, support but do not alone establish a diagnosis.

## Clinical patterns across diseases
The source documents show that diagnostic workup differs by disease, but follows the same principle of combining evidence:
- In **osteoarthritis**, diagnosis relies heavily on symptoms, exam, and imaging.
- In **rheumatoid arthritis**, classification depends on joint pattern, serology, inflammation markers, and symptom duration.
- In **spondylosis**, history, exam, and imaging are used together to identify degenerative change and complications such as spinal stenosis.

This makes diagnostic workup a general clinical framework rather than a disease-specific test.
