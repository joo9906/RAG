# Autoimmune Disease

An **autoimmune disease** is a condition in which the immune system mistakenly recognizes the body's own tissues as harmful and mounts an immune response against them. Instead of protecting the body from external threats, the immune system targets self-antigens, leading to [[concepts/chronic-inflammation|chronic inflammation]], tissue injury, and impaired organ function.

## Core idea

- The immune system loses normal **self-tolerance**.
- Immune cells and inflammatory mediators attack healthy tissues.
- Disease manifestations depend on the organ or tissue targeted.
- Autoimmune diseases may be **organ-specific** or **systemic**.
- Ongoing immune injury can lead to **progressive structural damage** and long-term disability.

## How autoimmune disease can present

Autoimmune disease does not always begin in the same way.

- Some patients develop **sudden systemic symptoms** before the main disease becomes obvious.
- Early features may include **fever**, **fatigue**, **loss of appetite**, **weight loss**, **depression**, and **anxiety**.
- Inflammatory disease may also begin with **polyarthritis**, signaling early joint involvement.
- Laboratory evidence of inflammation may be present even when the diagnosis is not yet clear.

These patterns show that autoimmune disease can produce both general constitutional symptoms and focused tissue-specific inflammation.

## Relationship to rheumatoid arthritis

The source documents [[summaries/2_1_1 24년 하반기 신입사원_조인스_이재은_학술교재_0011]], [[summaries/2_1_1 24년 하반기 신입사원_조인스_이재은_학술교재_0012]], [[summaries/2_1_1 24년 하반기 신입사원_조인스_이재은_학술교재_0013]], and [[summaries/2_1_1 24년 하반기 신입사원_조인스_이재은_학술교재_0014]] use **rheumatoid arthritis (RA)** as a key example of autoimmune disease.

In RA:

- The immune system attacks the **joints**, especially the **synovium**.
- Persistent immune activation causes **chronic inflammatory synovitis**.
- Ongoing inflammation leads to **cartilage destruction**.
- In advanced disease, inflammation contributes to **bone erosion**, **joint fusion**, and **stiffness**.
- **Pannus** formation is a key pathological feature, where proliferating synovial tissue and inflammatory cells invade surrounding structures.
- Clinically, RA often affects the **small joints of the hands, wrists, and feet**, but may also involve the **elbows, shoulders, hips, knees, and ankles**.
- A common symptom is **morning stiffness** lasting more than 30 minutes and sometimes much longer.
- RA may also produce **extra-articular** disease, including **vasculitis**, **pulmonary fibrosis**, **conjunctivitis**, **pericarditis**, and **lymphadenitis**.
- Diagnosis is based on a **multi-domain scoring system** that combines joint involvement, serology, acute-phase reactants, and symptom duration; a score of **6 or more** supports RA.
- **Rheumatoid factor (RF)** is positive in many RA patients, but it is not specific because it can also be positive in healthy people.
- Higher RF levels are associated with a tendency toward **more severe joint damage**.
- **ESR** and **CRP** are used to assess inflammatory activity.
- **CBC, liver function tests, and kidney function tests** help monitor treatment toxicity and possible organ involvement.

## Treatment themes in autoimmune disease

Autoimmune diseases often require treatment that controls inflammation and limits progressive damage.

- **NSAIDs** may be used to reduce **pain** and **inflammation**.
- **Steroids** provide strong short-term **anti-inflammatory** effects.
- **DMARDs** are used to slow disease progression and induce remission in diseases such as RA.
- **Biologic agents**, including **TNF inhibitors**, target inflammatory mediators more specifically.

In RA specifically, treatment goals are to:

- prevent and control **joint damage**
- suppress **inflammation** to prevent complications
- maintain daily function and quality of life
- reduce long-term disability and mortality

These treatments reflect a central principle of autoimmune disease management: controlling the immune response can reduce both symptoms and irreversible tissue injury.

## Laboratory and diagnostic themes

Autoimmune diseases often require a combination of clinical assessment and laboratory testing.

- **Serologic tests** may support diagnosis, but are rarely definitive on their own.
- **Acute-phase reactants** such as **ESR** and **CRP** reflect inflammatory burden.
- Routine blood tests such as **CBC** and organ function panels help detect treatment adverse effects and systemic involvement.
- A positive antibody or serologic marker must always be interpreted in the context of symptoms and exam findings.

In RA specifically, diagnosis is strengthened by the combination of clinical arthritis, serology, inflammatory markers, and disease duration rather than any single test.

## Key pathological themes

Autoimmune diseases often share several mechanisms:

- **Loss of self-tolerance**
- **Chronic inflammation**
- **Tissue-specific damage**
- **Progressive structural injury** in severe or untreated cases
- **Systemic manifestations** in diseases that affect multiple organs
- **Laboratory signs of inflammation** such as elevated ESR and CRP

In RA, these processes are seen clearly in the joint environment, where immune-mediated synovial inflammation drives joint destruction and can extend beyond the joints to other organs.

## Why this concept matters

Understanding autoimmune disease helps explain:

- why inflammation may persist without infection
- how the immune system can damage normal tissue
- why some diseases become chronic and progressive
- how pathological structures like [[concepts/pannus]] contribute to tissue destruction
- why some autoimmune conditions produce both joint symptoms and [[concepts/extra-articular-manifestations|extra-articular manifestations]]
- why inflammatory markers and serology must be interpreted as part of a broader diagnostic picture
