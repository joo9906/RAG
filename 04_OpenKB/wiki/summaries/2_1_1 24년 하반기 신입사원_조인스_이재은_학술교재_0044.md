# Summary: Osteoarthritis (OA)

This document explains the basic structure of joints and cartilage, the biochemical imbalance behind cartilage damage in OA, and the core clinical features, diagnosis, and treatment principles for osteoarthritis. It also notes the 2019 ACR guideline approach to OA management.

## Joint and cartilage basics

A **joint** is the place where two or more bones connect. It is composed of structures such as:

- **Cartilage**: protects the ends of bones and reduces friction
- **Ligament**: connects bones and helps control movement
- **Synovial membrane**: lines the joint and produces synovial fluid
- **Synovial fluid**: fills the joint cavity and reduces friction

**Cartilage** is elastic, lacks blood vessels and nerves, and functions as a protective cushion at the ends of bones.

### Main cartilage components

- **Collagen**: especially type II collagen in articular cartilage; provides elasticity
- **Proteoglycan**: major matrix component that supports fluidity and shock absorption
- **Chondrocyte**: cartilage cell responsible for growth and maintenance

## Cartilage damage and repair balance

The document emphasizes the balance between **cartilage synthesis** and **cartilage degradation**. In OA, this balance is disrupted.

Key mediators include:

- **MMPs (matrix metalloproteinases)**: major enzymes involved in cartilage breakdown
  - MMP-1
  - MMP-2
  - MMP-3
- **Cytokines**: especially IL-1 and TNF-α, which support MMP activity
- **TIMPs (tissue inhibitors of metalloproteinases)**: suppress MMP activity

When MMPs and TIMPs become imbalanced, cartilage tissue is progressively destroyed. This relates to [[concepts/cartilage-degeneration]] and [[concepts/inflammation-in-osteoarthritis]].

## Definition of osteoarthritis

**Osteoarthritis (OA)** is the most common form of arthritis. It is a degenerative joint disease in which articular cartilage wears away, exposing subchondral bone and causing inflammation of the synovial membrane, pain, and deformity.

It is also referred to as:

- degenerative arthritis
- degenerative joint disease

## Causes and risk factors

The document distinguishes between **primary OA** and **secondary OA**.

### Primary (idiopathic) OA

The exact cause is unknown, but contributing factors include:

- aging
- sex
- genetic factors
- obesity

### Secondary OA

Secondary OA occurs due to joint damage from trauma, disease, or deformity. Examples include:

- cartilage destruction after bacterial or tuberculous arthritis
- OA after major impact or repeated minor trauma

## Symptoms

Common OA symptoms include:

- pain related to joint use or activity
- stiffness, especially morning stiffness under 30 minutes
- limited range of motion
- joint swelling
- joint sounds
- joint deformity

These features are central to [[concepts/osteoarthritis-clinical-features]].

## Diagnosis

OA is diagnosed by combining:

- detailed patient history
- clinical findings
- physical examination
- radiologic findings

### X-ray findings

Radiographs may be normal early in disease, but as OA progresses they may show:

- narrowing of the joint space
- increased subchondral bone density (sclerosis)
- osteophyte formation at joint margins
- irregular joint surfaces

### MRI

MRI can help evaluate cartilage thickness, volume, and biochemical changes, making it useful for observing cartilage alteration. However, it is limited by cost and time.

## Treatment principles

The treatment goals are:

- reduce pain
- maintain joint function and prevent deformity
- improve quality of life

### Non-drug therapy

The document identifies non-pharmacologic management as a core treatment strategy:

- exercise
- weight control
- muscle strengthening

### Drug therapy

Examples include:

- **Topical agents**: NSAIDs and similar drugs
- **Oral agents**: acetaminophen, NSAIDs, COX-2 inhibitors
- **Intra-articular injections**: hyaluronic acid, steroids

The document notes that current non-drug and drug treatments are mainly **symptom-targeted**, not causal. It also states that no medication has yet been proven to prevent structural degeneration related to OA progression.

### Treatment categories

- **SYRADOA**: symptomatic rapid-acting drugs in OA, such as acetaminophen, NSAIDs, and intra-articular corticosteroids
- **SYSADOA**: symptomatic slow-acting drugs in OA, such as glucosamine/chondroitin sulfate, avocado/soybean unsaponifiables, diacerin, and Joins®
- **DMOADs**: drugs intended to modify OA progression by reducing structural change

This section is relevant to [[concepts/oa-treatment-strategies]] and [[concepts/disease-modifying-osteoarthritis-drugs]].

## 2019 ACR guideline

The document briefly introduces the **2019 ACR (American College of Rheumatology) guideline** for OA, which organizes treatment options for hand, knee, and hip OA into:

- **strongly recommended**
- **conditionally recommended**

It highlights common options such as exercise, weight loss, NSAIDs, and intra-articular steroids.

## Key takeaway

OA is a common degenerative joint disorder driven by progressive cartilage breakdown and an imbalance between cartilage synthesis and degradation. Diagnosis is based on symptoms, exam, and imaging, while treatment focuses on symptom relief, preserving function, and slowing functional decline through both non-pharmacologic and pharmacologic approaches.
